What is Family Therapy?

Family therapy (also called family counseling or family systems therapy) is a form of psychotherapy that involves multiple family members working together with a trained therapist to improve family dynamics, resolve conflicts, and address mental health or behavioral issues affecting the family system.

Unlike individual therapy, which focuses on one person's thoughts and behaviors, family therapy views problems through a relational lens—examining how family members interact, communicate, and influence each other. Sessions typically include parents, children, siblings, and sometimes extended family members, depending on the specific issues being addressed.

Common Family Issues We Address in Dearborn

Families seek therapy for many reasons. Our family therapists at Healing Therapy Center in Dearborn help families work through:

Parent-Teen Conflict & Behavioral Problems

Adolescence is challenging for both teens and parents. If your teenager is defiant, withdrawn, struggling with school, or engaging in risky behaviors, family therapy helps improve parent-teen communication, set appropriate boundaries, and address underlying issues driving problematic behavior. We help families navigate this difficult developmental stage while maintaining connection and mutual respect.

Divorce & Co-Parenting Challenges

Divorce profoundly affects children and family dynamics. Family therapy helps families adjust to divorce, process difficult emotions, establish healthy co-parenting relationships, and support children through this major transition. We work with both intact families experiencing marital stress and separated/divorced families learning to co-parent effectively.

Blended Family & Stepfamily Adjustment

Creating a blended family involves merging different family cultures, parenting styles, and loyalties. Common challenges include stepparent-stepchild relationships, favoritism concerns, discipline disagreements, and loyalty conflicts. Our therapists help blended families establish new family identities, set clear boundaries, and build cohesive units that honor all relationships involved.

Sibling Rivalry & Conflict

While some sibling conflict is normal, intense or persistent fighting can create a stressful home environment and cause lasting damage to sibling relationships. Family therapy helps siblings develop healthier conflict resolution skills, parents learn to mediate fairly, and families address underlying dynamics contributing to rivalry.

Mental Health Issues Affecting the Family

When one family member struggles with depression, anxiety, ADHD, autism, eating disorders, or other mental health conditions, the entire family is impacted. Family therapy educates family members about the condition, improves support systems, reduces family stress, and helps everyone develop effective coping strategies.

Substance Abuse & Addiction

Addiction is a family disease. When one member struggles with substance abuse, family members often develop enabling behaviors, codependency, or their own mental health issues. Family therapy is a critical component of addiction recovery, helping families establish healthy boundaries, improve communication, rebuild trust, and support lasting recovery.

Grief & Loss

The death of a family member, serious illness, or other significant loss affects each family member differently. Family therapy provides a safe space to process grief together, support each other through mourning, and find healthy ways to honor loss while moving forward as a family.

Life Transitions & Major Changes

Major life changes—relocation, job loss, retirement, new baby, child leaving for college, serious illness—can disrupt family equilibrium. Family therapy helps families navigate these transitions together, adjust to new roles and routines, and emerge stronger from change.

Communication Breakdown

When family members can't talk without yelling, shutting down, or arguing, everyday family life becomes exhausting. We teach families effective communication skills, active listening techniques, and conflict resolution strategies that reduce tension and increase understanding.

Multigenerational Conflict

Conflicts with extended family members—grandparents, in-laws, adult children—can create significant stress. Family therapy helps establish healthy boundaries, improve communication across generations, and resolve long-standing family patterns or disagreements.

Evidence-Based Family Therapy Approaches We Use

Our family therapists in Dearborn are trained in multiple therapeutic approaches and select methods based on each family's unique needs:

Structural Family Therapy

This approach examines family organization, hierarchies, boundaries, and subsystems (parent-child, sibling, marital). Structural family therapy is particularly effective when children have behavioral problems or when family boundaries are unclear (overly rigid or overly loose). The therapist helps families reorganize in healthier ways, establish appropriate parental authority, and create clear, flexible boundaries.

Systemic Family Therapy

Systemic therapy views problems as arising from family interaction patterns rather than individual pathology. This approach examines how family members' behaviors influence and maintain problems, and helps families develop new, healthier patterns of relating. It's especially useful when the "identified patient" (the family member seen as "the problem") is actually responding to broader family dynamics.

Emotionally Focused Family Therapy (EFFT)

Based on attachment theory, EFFT helps family members understand the emotional needs and attachment patterns driving their interactions. By recognizing these deeper emotional dynamics, families develop more secure, responsive relationships. This approach is particularly effective for parent-child relationships, adolescent issues, and families healing from trauma.

Narrative Therapy

Narrative therapy helps families "re-author" problematic stories they tell about themselves and each other. Instead of "John is the bad kid" or "Our family is dysfunctional," families develop more empowering, solution-focused narratives that open possibilities for change. This approach respects family members' expertise about their own lives and emphasizes strengths over pathology.

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT)

For families seeking practical solutions to specific problems, SFBT emphasizes strengths, resources, and envisioning desired futures rather than analyzing problems. This goal-oriented approach helps families identify what's working, build on successes, and make concrete changes quickly.

Who Attends Family Therapy Sessions?

This depends on the specific issues and treatment goals. Common configurations include:

  • Nuclear Family Sessions: Parents and children (most common)
  • Parental Subsystem: Just parents, to address parenting strategies or marital issues affecting children
  • Parent-Child Dyad: One parent with one child to address specific relationship issues
  • Sibling Subsystem: Siblings without parents to address sibling relationships
  • Extended Family: Including grandparents, stepparents, or other significant family members when relevant

Your family therapist will recommend the most effective session format based on your family's needs and may vary who attends across different sessions.

What to Expect in Family Therapy at Our Dearborn Office

First Session: Family Assessment

The initial family therapy session (60-90 minutes) involves the therapist meeting with relevant family members to understand family structure, relationship patterns, presenting problems, and therapy goals. The therapist will observe how family members communicate and interact. Some therapists meet briefly with individuals separately to understand each person's perspective.

Creating Safety & Ground Rules

Effective family therapy requires a safe environment where all family members feel comfortable sharing honestly. Your therapist will establish ground rules about respectful communication, ensure everyone has opportunities to be heard, and manage interruptions or disrespectful behavior.

Ongoing Sessions: Building New Patterns

Family therapy sessions are typically weekly, lasting 50-60 minutes (sometimes longer for larger families). Sessions involve discussing family issues, practicing new communication skills, identifying problematic patterns, processing emotions, and developing solutions together. Your therapist may assign "homework" between sessions to practice new behaviors at home.

Active Participation from All Members

Family therapy works best when all members participate actively and honestly. While one family member may have initiated therapy, meaningful change requires everyone's engagement. Your therapist remains neutral, supporting the family system rather than siding with any individual.

Does Family Therapy Work?

Research demonstrates that family therapy is highly effective for a wide range of issues:

  • Adolescent behavioral problems: Family therapy is more effective than individual therapy alone for teen substance abuse, delinquency, and behavioral disorders
  • Eating disorders: Family-based treatment is the most effective approach for adolescent anorexia and bulimia
  • Childhood mental health: Family involvement significantly improves treatment outcomes for childhood anxiety, depression, and ADHD
  • Relationship improvement: 65-75% of families report significant improvement in family functioning after family therapy
  • Treatment adherence: Family therapy increases compliance with medical and psychiatric treatment for chronic illnesses

Family Therapy vs. Individual Therapy: Which is Right?

Sometimes families wonder whether their child (or another family member) needs individual therapy, family therapy, or both. Here's guidance:

Consider Family Therapy When:

  • Problems involve family relationships or communication
  • One member's issue is affecting the entire family
  • Behavioral problems seem related to family dynamics
  • Major family changes (divorce, blended family, loss) are occurring
  • Family conflict is ongoing and unresolved

Consider Individual Therapy When:

  • One person has a specific mental health diagnosis needing individual treatment
  • Personal trauma or issues exist outside family context
  • An individual needs a private space to work through personal issues

Many families benefit from BOTH individual therapy and family therapy, with family members attending their own sessions while also participating in family sessions.

Frequently Asked Questions About Family Therapy

Does everyone in the family have to attend?

Not necessarily. While having all relevant family members attend is often ideal, therapy can still be effective if one member can't or won't participate. Your therapist will work with whoever is willing to attend and can create change within the family system even when everyone isn't present.

What if my child refuses to go to therapy?

This is common, especially with teenagers. Start by attending with the willing family members—seeing positive changes may encourage the reluctant member to join later. Your therapist can also provide strategies for engaging resistant family members. Sometimes starting with individual parent sessions or parent-child sessions (instead of full family) is less threatening.

Will the therapist take sides or blame someone?

Ethical family therapists remain neutral and view problems as relational patterns rather than any individual's fault. The goal is understanding how the family system operates, not assigning blame. Everyone contributes to family patterns, and everyone can contribute to change.

How long does family therapy take?

This varies greatly depending on the issues being addressed. Some families see improvement in 8-12 sessions for focused problems, while families dealing with complex trauma, severe behavioral issues, or long-standing patterns may benefit from 6+ months of work. Your therapist will regularly assess progress and adjust as needed.

What age do children need to be to participate?

Children as young as 3-4 can participate in family therapy when therapists use developmentally appropriate, play-based techniques. Older children and teenagers can engage in verbal processing. Your therapist will adapt methods to each family member's developmental level.

Can family therapy help if parents are divorcing?

Absolutely. Family therapy helps divorcing parents establish healthy co-parenting relationships, support children through the transition, and minimize the negative impact of divorce on children. Even after divorce, you're still a family—just a different structure.

Does insurance cover family therapy in Michigan?

Many insurance plans do cover family therapy, especially when there's a diagnosable mental health condition. Coverage varies by plan and sometimes depends on who is listed as the "identified patient." Our office can help verify your benefits when you call (313) 654-1915.
